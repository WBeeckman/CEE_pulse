Czechia CZK

Rate 3.75% (held 6 Aug) · Inflation (Jul) 1.7% y/y · Core ~2.8% · H1 budget deficit CZK 183.6bn
CNB held at 3.75% on 6 August (2W repo 3.75%, discount 2.75%, Lombard 4.75%), pausing after June's hike. Hungary HUF

Rate 5.50% (cut Aug) · Inflation (Jul) ~1.2% y/y · 2026 deficit guidance >7% GDP
MNB cut to 5.50% in August (−25bp, third straight cut), continuing the easing cycle as inflation stayed contained. The bigger story now is structural: Hungary has flagged a planned change to its inflation target as a step toward eventual eurozone membership, with the evaluation to conclude this autumn. That matters for rates — a lower target would limit the scope for further easing, and local reports of "no cut in September" on this basis strengthened the forint. ING still sees cuts in the pipeline (5.0% end-2026 call); Erste concurs. The September meeting is the key test.
MNB / ING / Erste — late Aug 2026 · Read source
Inflation among the EU's lowest. July CPI eased to around 1.2% y/y, extending several months of disinflation and giving the MNB room to keep cutting; August inflation (due 8 Sep) is expected to tick back up slightly to ~1.4% (ING). The contained backdrop is what has let the MNB run its cutting cycle even through the oil shock — though a planned inflation-target change could reset the calculus.
KSH / Erste (CEE inflation roundup) — 7 Aug 2026 · Read source
Energy emergency — Paks near total shutdown: the record-low Danube (≈10cm in Budapest) has cut Hungary's Paks nuclear plant to about 10% of capacity (240MW of 2,000MW), and state utility MVM now calls a complete shutdown "inevitable" — the first in the plant's 44 years. Paks supplies nearly half of Hungary's electricity; Magyar says it can run only until "Monday or Tuesday" and has warned of possible power cuts to large industrial users (sparing households, hospitals and utilities). Hungary is a net power importer, so the shortfall must be bought in at peak evening prices amid a 38°C+ heatwave.

◐ Record drought (status as of early Aug — update owed): This theme reflects the early-August peak; seasonal cooling into September likely eased the acute phase, but a fresh check is owed. Europe's worst drought in years pushed the Danube to its lowest level ever recorded — about 10cm in Budapest on 3 August, below the 2018 record — with no rain forecast and another 38°C+ heatwave. As of 8 Aug the emergency is deepening, not easing — no significant rain is forecast and another 38°C+ heatwave is bearing down, with the crisis now spanning three fronts:

① Energy — now critical. Hungary's Paks (≈half the country's electricity) is running at only ~10% of capacity — 240MW of 2,000MW — and operator MVM calls a full shutdown, the first in the plant's 44 years, "inevitable"; PM Magyar says it can run only until "Monday or Tuesday" and has flagged possible outages for large industrial users. Romania declared a nationwide energy state of alert for all of August: it shut Cernavodă unit 1, blasted 180kg of explosives through the Parjoaia cliff to divert Danube flow to the plant, and is importing evening-peak power from Ukraine — Bolojan warns losing unit 2 would cut ~20% of national output. Bulgaria's Kozloduy (its only nuclear plant, ~a third of its power) is on alert but still running normally via a deep-bay pumping station; Serbia is easing hydropower output. This is a once-through-cooling design flaw that recurs every heatwave (2003, 2022, 2023, now).

② Food & grain logistics. The Danube is the region's grain-export artery. Barges run at 10–40% of capacity (some 15–20%), forcing extra voyages and raising freight rates at peak harvest. Hungarian wheat is seen ~1m t below normal; Czech yields could be 10–40% lower, with insurers refusing cherries/apricots; Romania (a top-3 EU grain exporter) has restricted irrigation water. And it reaches beyond the Danube basin: Poland has issued 61 drought warnings covering about half the country, with the Vistula near historic lows and winter-wheat/rapeseed losses estimated at 10%+ — a hit to another of the EU's major grain and oilseed exporters.

③ Fuel & transport. Low water cut fuel-barge capacity by ~half in places, forcing costlier road haulage — an inflation risk on top of the (now easing) oil shock.

The through-line: CEE infrastructure built for historical climate patterns is failing under repeated extremes, and the fiscal room to adapt is exactly what the region's deficit procedures (five of six countries) constrain.
